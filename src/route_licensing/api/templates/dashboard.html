Journey coverage — determines the verdict
Every stop pair A→B on the proposed route is checked against the existing network in three tiers. The analysis stops at the first tier that gives a result — stronger coverage tiers carry more weight.
Tier 1 — Direct
An existing bus already makes the same journey at the same time of day. A direct service is the strongest duplication signal — indicates High overlap or Moderate overlap depending on coverage level.
Fraction of A→B pairs directly served. Default 80%.
Fraction of A→B pairs directly served. Default 60%. Also used as the Moderate overlap threshold for Tiers 2 and 3.
Tier 2 — Interchange
No direct service, but the passenger could board an existing bus at A, transfer at an intermediate stop, and reach B on a second existing service within a 5 to 45 minute connection window. An interchange is a real but less convenient alternative — indicates Moderate overlap only, not High overlap.
→ Moderate overlap if Tier 2 ≥ 60%
Mirrors the Tier 1 Moderate threshold above. Tier 2 cannot trigger High overlap.
Tier 3 — Walk access
No direct or interchange service, but the passenger could walk to a nearby stop and catch an existing bus to their destination. The weakest coverage signal — Moderate overlap only.
→ Moderate overlap if Tier 3 ≥ 60%
Mirrors the Tier 1 Moderate threshold above. Walk access cannot trigger High overlap.
City centre: 200–300 m · Suburban: 400 m · Rural: 600–900 m
Demand signal — only applies if a passenger demand file is uploaded
Without a demand file these thresholds have no effect on the overlap assessment. When demand data is present: high demand on an infrequent existing service (capacity gap) softens High overlap to Moderate; low demand on overlapping corridors can elevate Low to Moderate overlap.
Passengers per hour at or above which existing demand is considered high — triggers capacity gap softening.
Passengers per hour below which demand is too low to justify a new competing service.
